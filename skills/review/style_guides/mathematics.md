# Mathematics Review Style Guide (applied-mathematics survey voice)

## Narrative voice

Mathematics reviews in this corpus occupy a wide register — from the archival monograph of Acta Numerica to the opinionated essay of Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics to the tightly scoped specialist survey of IEEE JSTSP — but they share a governing tone: confident, pedagogically oriented, and first-person-plural when the author stakes a position. Temperature is predominantly neutral; warmth enters in the pedagogical monographs (Davenport et al.'s compressed sensing chapter, Vempala and Diakonikolas's percolation survey) and polemics in the essay form (Donoho's "50 Years"). Paragraphs are long and grammatically deliberate, each one advancing a single claim through its own internal deductive arc rather than cataloguing references. The reader is always being led toward a synthesis, never merely handed a bibliography.

## Opening conventions

Mathematics reviews open almost exclusively with one of two moves: a field-landscape statement that names the subject's scope and institutional moment, or a puzzle-or-paradox that announces the core tension the paper will resolve. Pure historical arcs are rarer than in physics reviews; when history appears, it tends to arrive as the second paragraph, after the claim. Roughly half of the corpus opens with a one-sentence thesis about the mathematical object under study ("Low-rank matrices arise in an incredibly wide range of settings throughout science and applied mathematics" — Davenport and Romberg, §1), and the other half opens with a sociological or motivational frame before narrowing to the mathematics ("We are in the midst of a digital revolution that is driving the development and deployment of new kinds of sensing systems" — Davenport et al., §1.1). First citations arrive by sentence three or four; introductory paragraphs run 100–150 words. A clear thesis sentence appears by the end of the first section's opening paragraph in every paper in the corpus.

## Section architecture

Monographs (>40pp) carry 8–20 top-level sections with short nominal-topic titles ("Sensing matrices", "Matrix completion", "Noise reduction methods"). Specialist reviews (15–40pp) carry 5–8 sections. All papers in the corpus use nominal-topic section titles rather than gerunds, full statements, or questions, with the single exception of Scholze's perfectoid survey, which uses an occasional full clause ("A p-adic analogue of Riemann's classification of complex abelian varieties"). An outlook or summary section appears in roughly two-thirds of the corpus, and when present it typically opens with a grand claim that recontextualizes the survey's contribution before listing open problems. Method/theory/algorithm splits — rather than historical periodization — are the most common organizational skeleton in the applied-mathematics papers; the pure-mathematics papers (percolation, perfectoid spaces) instead organize around a logical deduction chain in which each section's main theorem is a lemma for the next.

## Thesis-per-section convention

Applied-mathematics sections lead with a claim about the mathematical landscape or the algorithm under discussion, not with a citation. Pure-mathematics sections lead with a precise mathematical statement that orients the reader to what is being proved. The following verbatim examples from the corpus illustrate the dominant pattern:

"The theory of low-rank recovery is clean and elegant when the measurement operator A(·) is a random projection. Applications where this is a good model for the observations are limited, but looking at it as an abstract problem gives us real mathematical insight about why low-rank recovery works." — Davenport and Romberg (IEEE JSTSP 2016, §4)

"We now turn our attention to the main focus of our study, namely, numerical algorithms for solving optimization problems that arise in large-scale machine learning." — Bottou et al. (SIAM Review 2018, §3)

"The original aim of the theory of perfectoid spaces was to prove Deligne's weight-monodromy conjecture over p-adic fields by reduction to the case of local fields of equal characteristic p, where the result is known." — Scholze (Current Dev. Mathematics 2012, §1)

The first two examples are characteristic of applied-mathematics surveys: they open with a claim about what the mathematics is like, then locate the exposition to follow. The Scholze example opens with the historical motivating problem, which is characteristic of pure-mathematics surveys where the key move is identifying the correct reduction.

## Synthesis moves

The five canonical techniques for unifying disparate work into a claim appear across the corpus.

**The unifying equation or operator.** A single mathematical object is introduced early and then tracked through every subsequent section, accumulating meaning as the model generalizes. Davenport and Romberg introduce the linear measurement operator y_i = ⟨X_0, A_i⟩ + z_i in §2 and then show that Gaussian projections, matrix completion, and phase retrieval are all special cases of the same A. Davenport et al. do the same with y = Ax in §1.4. This move is the applied-mathematics equivalent of the unifying Hamiltonian in physics reviews.

**The taxonomy tree.** A field is classified into a hierarchy of sub-problems before any are solved. Bottou et al. preview three classes of advanced methods at the close of §3 and then give each a full section; Davenport et al. introduce sparse models, finite unions, analog unions, low-rank models, and manifold models in §1.3 as a structured menu before addressing measurement. The taxonomy is always announced explicitly ("Our survey focuses on three basic variations of this measurement model" — Davenport and Romberg, §2).

**The contrast pair.** Two approaches, regimes, or cultural positions are placed in formal opposition, each clarified by proximity to the other. Donoho's "50 Years" uses Breiman's two cultures (generative vs. predictive) as its spine; Bottou et al. contrast stochastic gradient and batch methods as their central organizing tension; Davenport et al. contrast the noise-free and noisy settings across §1.5.1 and §1.5.2.

**The running worked example.** A single concrete instance — a text-classification problem, a 3×3 system of quadratic equations, a polynomial over a p-adic field — is introduced early and then periodically revisited as new tools are applied to it, so abstract machinery always has a visible anchor. Scholze's polynomial example in §2.1 ("Say M is the extension of K^♭ given by adjoining a root of X^2 − 7tX + t^5…") runs for four paragraphs to ground the tilting construction.

**The head-to-head comparison with recommendation.** Algorithm surveys close subsections with an explicit comparison of competing methods followed by a practical recommendation. "It tends to outperform nuclear norm minimization [haldar09ra, jain13lo], especially in cases where the rank R is very small compared to N, M" (Davenport and Romberg, §3.3) is the template: a quantified advantage followed by the regime in which it holds.

## Transition moves

Between-section transitions in mathematics reviews fall into three functional types. The most common is the bridge, which restates the previous section's conclusion and announces the narrower or broader question that follows: "With these case studies in hand, we turn our attention to the latter two questions on optimization algorithms, the discussions around which represent the bulk of the paper" (Bottou et al., §2→§3). The contrast pivot names what the preceding theory cannot do before announcing the harder setting: "While this provides some insight into the kind of behavior we can hope for in many applications, it is also far from representative of the type of observations we often encounter in practice" (Davenport and Romberg, §4→§5). The historical pivot announces a self-conscious change in register or a switch of companion paper: "This introduction is essentially identical to the introduction to [29]" (Scholze, §3). Transitions at the paragraph level are typically a single sentence ending on the new topic noun, serving as a topic sentence for the paragraph that follows rather than a separate interstitial paragraph.

## Citation voice

Citation practice splits cleanly by register. Archival monographs and specialist reviews (SIAM Review, Acta Numerica, IEEE JSTSP) use parenthetical citation almost exclusively — author names rarely appear in prose, and citations are collected at the end of claims: "recent advances in the closely related field of compressive sensing [Donoho 2006, Candès 2006, Davenport et al. 2011] have enabled a burst of progress." Citation density is high: 3–5 citations per paragraph in the body, rising to 6–8 per paragraph in sections reviewing algorithmic alternatives. Essay-register papers (Donoho's "50 Years") use narrative citation heavily — 60–70% of citations name the author in prose — and carry a much lower density (1–2 per paragraph) because they are arguing about the significance of work rather than cataloguing it. Named citations in the applied-mathematics body are reserved for landmark results: Candès and Romberg, Recht, Donoho and Tanner appear by name; everyone else is parenthetical. The pure-mathematics papers (Scholze) sit between these poles, naming specific theorem-prover pairs frequently ("due to Fontaine-Wintenberger, [15]") while keeping supporting citations parenthetical.

## Anti-stacking discipline

The dominant strategy for avoiding authorship-leading stacks is to open each paragraph with a claim about the mathematical object or phenomenon, then weave the literature into the argument as parenthetical support or mid-paragraph attribution. From Davenport and Romberg: "Computing the best low-rank approximation, then, is akin to thresholding the singular values: we take the matrix, compute its SVD, keep the large singular values while killing off the small ones, and then reconstruct" — the SVD algorithm is described as a property of the mathematical object, not as something Eckart and Young invented. Similarly, from Davenport et al.: "Signals can often be well-approximated as a linear combination of just a few elements from a known basis or dictionary. When this representation is exact we say that the signal is sparse." — the definition leads, not any author's name. From Bottou et al.: "A major theme of this work is that large-scale machine learning represents a distinctive setting in which traditional nonlinear optimization techniques typically falter." — the claim precedes any citation, and the literature appears only to document what was already asserted. The one authorship-leading pattern that does appear — and is harder to avoid — is the historical introduction of named algorithms: "The field of CS grew out of the work of Candès, Romberg, and Tao and of Donoho, who showed that a finite-dimensional signal having a sparse or compressible representation can be recovered from a small set of linear, non-adaptive measurements" (Davenport et al., §1.1). The distinction is that this naming occurs in the historical framing of the introduction, not in the technical body, and the named authors are immediately followed by the algorithmic implication, not a summary of their results.

## Figure and equation roles

Equations are the primary argumentative medium in all but the essay-register papers. Density ranges from 0.1 equations per page (Donoho) to 5 per page (Davenport et al.), with most papers in the 3–4 per page range. Every equation in the applied-mathematics papers is numbered when it is a definition or result that will be referenced later; intermediate steps in proofs are often unnumbered. Equations are re-referenced explicitly and frequently by number: "(1.12)", "(eq:matrixlasso)" — the paper is designed for non-sequential reading. Figures serve three roles in this corpus: conceptual-geometry figures that make a claim about norm behavior or signal geometry (l_p unit spheres, union-of-subspaces diagrams); overview schematics illustrating the relationship between measurement model and recovery algorithm; and sparsity-as-visual-example figures (wavelet coefficient images). The pure-mathematics papers carry no figures. Boxes and sidebars are absent from the entire corpus; the pedagogical machinery is carried entirely by the prose and equation structure.

## Signature moves

1. **Announce the taxonomy before the content.** Every applied-mathematics survey in this corpus previews its classification of the subject — three observation models, four algorithm families, six activities of data science — before developing any of them, so the reader holds a map while navigating the territory.
2. **Track one unifying object.** Introduce the key mathematical object (the linear operator A, the empirical risk function R_n, the nuclear norm ‖X‖_*) in the first technical section and allow it to accumulate specializations and interpretations throughout, so the survey reads as the deepening of a single concept rather than a catalogue of related topics.
3. **Close algorithm comparisons with a practical regime statement.** Rather than declaring one method "better," state the regime in which each method wins: "tends to outperform nuclear norm minimization… especially in cases where the rank R is very small." This gives the reader actionable guidance without overstating the theory.
4. **Reserve named citation for landmarks; treat everything else parenthetically.** Candès, Donoho, Recht, Scholze, Faltings appear by name because their results are the pivots on which the exposition turns. Supporting citations documenting a technique or confirming a bound are collected parenthetically at the sentence's close.
5. **Make the reduction explicit.** In both pure and applied mathematics, the climactic move is showing that a hard problem reduces to an easier one — quadratic equations to rank-1 matrix recovery, mixed-characteristic geometry to equal-characteristic geometry, noisy recovery to noise-free recovery plus a perturbation bound. State the reduction as a sentence-level claim before the theorem and again in the transition to the next section.
