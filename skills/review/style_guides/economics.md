# Economics Review Style Guide (AEA/Handbook house voice)

## Narrative voice

Economics reviews are simultaneously analytical and argumentative: the prevailing mood is neutral but purposeful, with a persistent undercurrent of claim-making that distinguishes the genre from pure survey. Voice ranges from first-person-plural in handbook chapters ("we proceed in four steps") to first-person-singular in prize lectures ("I begin with the fundamental problem"), but both registers share the same rhetorical posture—quantitative confidence paired with explicit normative framing. Paragraphs are long and built on a claim-evidence-implication rhythm, rarely allowing a paragraph to close without telling the reader what to conclude. Temperature is controlled: polemic, when it appears, is delivered through precision rather than heat—Paris is "uncoordinated and voluntary," not "a failure."

## Opening conventions

The majority of economics reviews open with a field-landscape or puzzle-or-paradox frame; historical arcs and big-number statistics appear but are less dominant than in natural-science reviews. A handbook chapter characteristically opens with a research question stated as a single sentence ("How important is household heterogeneity for the amplification and propagation of macroeconomic shocks?") before immediately narrowing scope along numbered dimensions. A prize lecture is permitted a wider aperture—one or two paragraphs surveying the breadth of the field—before converging on the paper's specific externality or mechanism. First paragraphs average 80–140 words. The first citation typically arrives in sentence four or five, not sentence one; the opening moves of claim and scope-narrowing are author-owned. A clear thesis sentence arrives by the end of the first full section (§1 or the abstract), not always by the end of ¶1.

## Section architecture

Handbook chapters run 6–10 numbered top-level sections with frequent subsections; specialist journal articles run 4–7; short articles (AEA papers and proceedings, AER lectures) run 2–5. Appendices carry robustness tables rather than supplementary narrative. Section titles are almost exclusively nominal-topic ("Calibration," "The Great Recession: a Heterogeneous Household Perspective," "From National to International Policies"); question titles appear occasionally in pedagogical JEP articles. An outlook or "future directions" subsection is present in handbook chapters but absent in prize lectures, which close with a policy call to action instead. Method, theory, and empirics are typically staged sequentially—facts first, model second, policy counterfactuals third—rather than interleaved.

## Thesis-per-section convention

Sections open with a claim or an orientation sentence that is a direct echo of the paper's abstract-level thesis, narrowed one step. Orientation sentences ("In this section we lay out the benchmark model") are more common in handbook chapters; claim sentences are more common in journal articles and lectures. Representative examples from the corpus:

"Automation — defined as capital replacing labor in tasks that workers used to perform — always reduces the labor share and can reduce employment." — Acemoglu & Restrepo (JEP 2019, §3 opening)

"The more severe consumption declines in economies with larger wealth inequality imply a smaller collapse in investment, and thus a faster recovery from the recession, although this last effect is quantitatively small." — Krueger, Mitman & Perri (HoM 2016, §6 mid-section)

"I begin with the fundamental problem posed by climate change—that it is a public good or externality." — Nordhaus (AER 2019, §I opening)

## Synthesis moves

The dominant synthesis move in economics reviews is the **comparative scorecard table**: a single table whose rows are policies, models, or subgroups and whose columns are outcome metrics. This table is built once and referenced throughout, so that all robustness checks, counterfactuals, and alternative assumptions can be read as rows added to the same scorecard. In Bernanke's monetary-policy appendix, Tables 5–7 replicate the same seven-metric structure across three expectation assumptions; in Nordhaus, Tables 1–2 present the social cost of carbon across nine scenarios and six discount rates. The table does the unification that prose alone cannot.

The second canonical move is the **unifying framework diagram**: a simple two-equation or flow-diagram representation that maps the entire argument onto a small number of variables. Nordhaus reduces DICE to max W = max ∫U[c(t)]e^{-ρt}dt subject to c(t) = M(·), making the damage-function and discount-rate debates visible as disputes about M. Acemoglu and Restrepo map task allocation to a unit interval with one moving threshold, and every subsequent theoretical result follows from shifting that threshold. These diagrams are introduced early and remain the interpretive grammar for the paper.

Third is the **contrast pair**: two scenarios, two time periods, or two model variants placed side by side so their difference is the finding. Krueger, Mitman & Perri's central result is the gap between the representative-agent consumption response and the heterogeneous-household response (0.5 percentage points); Nordhaus's decarbonization tables contrast the world with and without China to show that apparent policy progress vanishes when China's structural change is stripped out.

Fourth is the **taxonomy with ranked alternatives**: policy options, modelling approaches, or mechanisms are listed, classified, and immediately rank-ordered. Nordhaus's Plan A/B/C taxonomy is the sharpest case—the taxonomy is introduced, all three options are defined, two are eliminated, and the reader is left with one—but the move recurs whenever the paper needs to justify a focus on one mechanism out of several.

## Transition moves

Economics transitions tend to be explicit and functional—announcing the logical relationship between sections rather than relying on thematic resonance. Characteristic forms:

"Having documented the salient features of the joint wealth, income, and consumption distribution in the United States prior to the Great Recession and their dynamics over the course of the downturn, we now proceed with a quantitative evaluation of how well standard economic theory can explain these patterns." — Krueger, Mitman & Perri (bridge)

"The analysis so far has focused on automation. We now turn to the other side of technological change: the creation of new tasks." — Acemoglu & Restrepo (contrast)

"So that leaves Plan A, abatement, as the only realistic option to deal with climate change." — Nordhaus (contrast, with implicit elimination)

"In light of the failure of past agreements, it is easy to conclude that international cooperation is doomed to failure. This is the wrong conclusion." — Nordhaus (contrast)

The zoom-in transition ("The analysis in §X made predictions. We now ask whether the data are consistent with those predictions.") is the theory-to-empirics workhorse and appears in nearly every AEA-style article.

## Citation voice

The dominant style is parenthetical: authors are named in the flow of prose only at landmark moments (Krusell and Smith (1998), Samuelson (1954)) and for recent papers the author is citing as close competitors; the majority of citations appear in terminal parentheses. Introductory footnotes carry survey citations that would otherwise interrupt the opening claims. Citation density in the body averages 2–3 per paragraph in handbook chapters and 1–2 in lecture-essays; introductions are denser. The practice of acknowledging own prior work by year (Nordhaus 1975, 1977, 1990, 1992…) as an intellectual chronology is distinctive to prize lectures and senior-scholar surveys; junior-author papers rarely self-cite in this cumulative way.

## Anti-stacking discipline

The strongest papers in this corpus open paragraphs with claims about phenomena—prices, distributions, mechanisms, failures—and weave authorship in parenthetically or mid-paragraph. The stacking pattern ("X et al. showed that…") is present but reserved for literature-survey subsections and footnotes. Representative claim-led openings:

"Net worth is by far the most concentrated variable, especially at the top of the distribution." — Krueger, Mitman & Perri (§2.2): the distributional claim precedes any mention of dataset or authors.

"Free-riding is a major hurdle in the solution of global externalities, and it lies at the heart of the failure to deal with climate change." — Nordhaus (§III.A): the mechanism is named before any country or agreement is cited.

A paragraph that does stack—"Krusell and Smith (1998) in turn build on stationary versions of the model with household wealth heterogeneity, and thus on Bewley (1986), Imrohoroglu (1989), Huggett (1993, 1997) and Aiyagari (1994)"—appears in footnotes or in dedicated lineage paragraphs, bracketed off from the claim-driven main text. Its function is intellectual genealogy, not argument.

## Figure and equation roles

The opening figure is almost never a data plot: it is either a conceptual framework (Nordhaus's circular flow of global warming science), a schematic (Acemoglu-Restrepo's task-space interval), or—in the prize-lecture case—a piece of fine art deployed as extended metaphor (Goya's Colossus). Data plots arrive in §2 and carry the dual burden of documenting empirical facts and providing calibration targets for the model. Tables outnumber figures; a well-formed economics review has two to five quantitative tables whose column structure is held constant across robustness checks.

Equation density is moderate—0.4 to 1.5 equations per page depending on the theory content. Equations are numbered and re-referenced by number in the text; they are introduced with one to two sentences of setup that state the economic interpretation before the formal expression, and followed by one to two sentences that explain what the equation means rather than what it contains. Boxes and sidebars are absent; footnotes absorb the material that would otherwise appear there.

## Signature moves

1. State the research question in the opening sentence, then immediately narrow scope along two or three explicitly numbered dimensions—this rhetorical move signals quantitative discipline before the first citation appears.
2. Build one master table or diagram early, hold its structure constant, and force all subsequent results and robustness checks to live as rows or variants within that structure—the table becomes an argument, not a summary.
3. Distinguish the paper's mechanism from a superficially similar alternative (automation vs. skill-biased change; wealth-poor non-hand-to-mouth vs. hand-to-mouth; voluntary Paris pledges vs. binding carbon clubs) and keep that contrast terminologically alive across every section.
4. Announce a multi-step roadmap in §1 and re-invoke it at each major transition, so the reader always knows which step is in progress—a device that substitutes for a discursive narrative thread in papers organized around sequential model extensions.
5. Close not with a literature gap but with a policy or welfare implication stated in quantitative terms: the output drop is 1 percentage point smaller, the consumption decline is 0.5 percentage points larger, the global carbon price under no tariff is one-tenth of the optimal level—leaving the reader with a number, not a direction for future research.
