# Earth & Environment Review Style Guide (assessment-to-essay house voice)

## Narrative voice

The dominant register is the *assessment*: authoritative without being pedantic, policy-aware without being polemical, quantitatively grounded without deploying equations as the primary persuasion device. Prose is declarative; paragraphs open on a phenomenon rather than on an author. Where urgency enters — as in Lenton et al. (Nature 2019) and Post et al. (Science Advances 2019) — it is licensed by accumulated evidence, not by rhetorical escalation. First-person plural ("we propose," "we demonstrate") appears at framing moments and at claim delivery, then recedes into passive for the evidentiary body. Paragraphs are self-contained but look ahead, closing on a synthesis beat or a named gap.

## Opening conventions

The corpus splits between two strategies. The *big-number anchor* leads with a planetary-scale quantity: "Permafrost underlies ~25% of the Northern Hemisphere land surface and stores an estimated ~1,700 Pg of carbon" (Schädel et al., Nat. Rev. Earth & Environ. 2022, §1). The *field-landscape* opener situates the paper in an identified gap: "The stability and resilience of the Earth system and human well-being are inseparably linked, yet their interdependencies are rarely captured in sustainability frameworks" (Richardson et al., Nature 2023, §1). Both plant the thesis in the first paragraph. First citations arrive by sentence two or three; opening paragraphs rarely exceed 130 words. Short-format papers (Nature commentary, PNAS letter) treat the abstract as the opener, foregrounding the claim in bold before the body begins.

## Section architecture

Short papers (4–6 pp.) use 3–5 sections; specialist reviews (12–29 pp.) use 6–10. Section-title voice is overwhelmingly *nominal topic* — "Projected Change," "Carbon Cycle Feedbacks" — with one notable exception: Post et al. (Science Advances 2019) structures every body section as a bold uppercase question ("HOW RAPIDLY IS ARCTIC SEA ICE DIMINISHING?"), turning the review into a policy-facing FAQ. All papers include an outlook or future-directions section; conclusion sections (2–5 paragraphs) close on governance implications, not on finding restatements. The Annual Review format adds a final "Carbon Cycle Surprises" section treating black-swan uncertainties explicitly — an epistemic-humility gesture absent from shorter formats.

## Thesis-per-section convention

Sections open with a claim about the phenomenon, never with an orientation sentence ("In this section, we discuss X"). The claim arrives in the first sentence or first independent clause, before any methodological hedging:

"Four of the nine planetary boundaries have now been crossed as a result of human activity." — Steffen et al. (Science 2015, §3)

"Environmental consequences of continued Arctic warming are unlikely to be limited to the northern high latitudes." — Post et al. (Science Advances 2019, §3)

"Current Earth system models systematically underestimate permafrost carbon release because they incompletely represent abrupt thaw processes." — Schädel et al. (Nat. Rev. Earth & Environ. 2022, §4)

## Synthesis moves

Five devices recur. The *unifying equation*: the carbon budget identity (EFOS + ELUC = GATM + SOCEAN + SLAND + BIM) organizes a 69-page paper, each section contributing one term (Friedlingstein et al., ESSD 2023); the Bayesian product delivers the same architecture for climate sensitivity (Sherwood et al., Rev. Geophys. 2020). The *taxonomy with hierarchy*: core vs. non-core planetary boundaries; cryosphere/biosphere/circulation tipping elements; gradual vs. abrupt permafrost thaw — introduced early and carried consistently to govern section depth. The *multi-method convergence panel*: three independent SOCEAN estimates side-by-side, divergence as a result not a caveat; in the human niche paper, crop, livestock, and economic productivity data all independently converge on ~13 °C. The *scenario matrix*: low/medium/high emission narratives paired with ecological stories (Schuur et al., Annu. Rev. 2022), carrying uncertainty through named scenarios rather than intervals alone. The *contrast-pair*: Arctic vs. Antarctic, gradual vs. abrupt, safe vs. just — two systems similar enough to share a paper, different enough to generate the argument.

## Transition moves

The bridge form dominates: a single pivot sentence naming what has been established and what follows.

"Having established the process constraints on individual feedbacks, we now turn to constraints from the historical climate record." — Sherwood et al. (Rev. Geophys. 2020, §3→§4)

"The type of thaw — gradual versus abrupt — determines both the rate and the form of carbon release." — Schädel et al. (Nat. Rev. Earth & Environ. 2022, §2→§3)

"These tipping elements don't exist in isolation — they interact." — Lenton et al. (Nature 2019, §1→§2)

The caveat transition precedes implications: "The transparency of our approach is appealing, but inevitably implies some loss of nuance" (Xu et al., PNAS 2020). Transitions close the previous section, never open the next. They are rarely longer than two sentences.

## Citation voice

Parenthetical citations dominate. Author names appear in prose only when a result is so landmark that the name is the claim ("Burke, Hsiang, and Miguel found a nonlinear economic optimum at ~13 °C") or when a prior framework is being explicitly superseded. Otherwise the paragraph stays claim-forward. Density varies widely: Nature/Science short pieces run 1.5–2.5 per paragraph; Annual Review and Reviews of Geophysics monographs run 4.5–5.5. Introductions carry the highest named-citation density, populating the field landscape before the argument opens.

## Anti-stacking discipline

Across the corpus, 55–75% of mid-paper paragraphs open with a claim about the phenomenon; fewer than 20% begin with an authorship phrase. Stacking ("X et al. found..., Y et al. showed...") appears almost exclusively in methods sections and introductions. In the body, authors are tucked parenthetically or mid-sentence. Three claim-leading openings from the corpus:

"The global atmospheric CO2 concentration averaged over 2022 reached 417.1 ± 0.1 ppm." — Friedlingstein et al. (ESSD 2023, §3)

"Abrupt thaw represents a threshold change that degrades permafrost significantly faster than gradual top-down warming alone." — Schuur et al. (Annu. Rev. 2022, §4)

"The risk of tipping cascades could be an existential threat to civilization." — Lenton et al. (Nature 2019, §2)

By contrast, an authorship-led opening: "Overland et al. found that the Arctic is expected to continue to warm much more rapidly than lower latitudes" (Post et al., Sci. Adv. 2019) — this pattern is reserved for model-intercomparison contexts where the projection's source is itself the result.

## Figure and equation roles

Figures serve four functions. The *overview schematic* (Figure 1 or 2): a single figure containing the entire system taxonomy — the nine-boundary radial spider diagram (Steffen et al.), the 15-tipping-element network map (Lenton et al.), the permafrost carbon-pool-to-atmosphere pathway (Schädel et al.) — referenced repeatedly throughout. The *scenario comparison panel*: time-series placing multiple models on one axis, where inter-method spread is itself the result. The *geographic projection*: current vs. future niche-suitability maps, sea-ice extent anomaly calendars — translating global mean quantities into spatially legible claims. The *cascade diagram*: tipping-element interaction networks, Bayesian DAGs, 3×3 scenario matrices. Equations are sparse except in Reviews of Geophysics monographs; when present they are definitional anchors, not derivations. Boxes appear in Nature Reviews and Annual Review formats as definitional scaffolds; absent in Science and PNAS.

## Signature moves

1. Open with a planetary-scale anchor number or a landmark gap sentence, plant the thesis before the paragraph ends, and let the first citation arrive parenthetically by sentence three — never begin by introducing an author.
2. Impose a binary or ternary taxonomy at the outset (gradual vs. abrupt thaw; safe vs. just boundaries; fast vs. slow tipping elements) and carry it symmetrically through every subsequent section, so that the reader always knows which side of the contrast they are reading.
3. Present multiple independent estimation methods for any key quantity and treat their convergence or divergence as a result, not as a methodological appendix — the honest budget imbalance, the three-source SOCEAN comparison, the triple causal-evidence argument for the human temperature niche are all examples of this move.
4. Convert uncertainty into scenario structure rather than into confidence intervals alone: low/medium/high narratives with ecological names carry uncertainty into the reader's imagination in a way that ±1σ ranges cannot, and they enable policy-legible reasoning without foreclosing scientific honesty.
5. Close on governance, cooperation, or human development implications rather than on research needs — earth-environment reviews earn their authority by translating Earth-system evidence into actionable stakes, and the final paragraph is where that translation is made explicit.
