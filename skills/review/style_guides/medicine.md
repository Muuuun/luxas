# Medicine Review Style Guide (NEJM / Nature Medicine house voice)

## Narrative voice

Medicine reviews read as authoritative briefings to an expert who is already fluent in the biology but needs to be oriented to the clinical stakes and the current state of evidence. The register is clinical synthesis: cool in analytic temperature, confident in its evaluative claims, but never polemical. Sentences are declarative rather than hedged, and paragraphs drive toward a clinical or mechanistic conclusion rather than toward a summary of the literature. In specialist journals (Science, Nature Medicine, Nature Reviews Drug Discovery) the voice is tighter and more technical; in NEJM it softens toward the clinician reader, with a warmer pedagogical inflection that permits vignette openers and first-person normative statements at conclusion.

## Opening conventions

Medicine reviews split sharply by venue and register. NEJM reviews are unusually willing to open with a patient vignette — a clinical scene that places the reader bedside before any scientific claim is made. Rajkomar et al. (NEJM 2019) open with a 49-year-old man presenting to the emergency department with altered mental status and septic physiology; the patient reappears in the conclusion, now rescued by machine-learning-assisted diagnosis. This frame is rare in other domains and signals the genre's persistent orientation toward clinical application. More commonly, specialist-journal reviews open with a field landscape that identifies a gap: "yet the same approach has consistently failed in solid tumours" (Hou et al., Nat. Rev. Drug Discov. 2021) or with a historical arc that converts prior failures into mechanistic prerequisites (Ribas & Wolchok, Science 2018, opening with Coley's 1890s bacterial infection observations). Short commentaries in NEJM may open with a presidential or institutional quote framing a policy initiative (Collins & Varmus, NEJM 2015). First citations arrive by sentence three to five in specialist reviews; NEJM reviews may delay them longer to preserve the narrative flow of the opening. A thesis sentence closing the first paragraph is the norm in specialist venues; NEJM reviews sometimes distribute the thesis across the first two paragraphs.

## Section architecture

Medicine reviews in specialist journals typically have six to eight top-level sections, with the specialist range (15–40 pages) dominating the corpus. Section titles are predominantly nominal phrases (CAR T Cell Malignancies, Toxic Effects, Key Challenges) or gerund phrases (Boosting T Cell Infiltration, Modulating Immunogenicity) depending on whether the section describes a domain or an engineering goal. Reviews in translational journals (Nature Reviews Drug Discovery, Nature Reviews Genetics) favor gerund titles that frame the section as an active problem-solving effort, while NEJM and Science reviews lean toward nominal topic titles that name the clinical or biological domain. An outlook or future-directions section is nearly universal. Reviews organized around a technology platform (CAR T cells, mRNA vaccines) typically interleave mechanism, optimization, clinical evidence, and toxicity in that order, following the logic of translational development; reviews organized around a clinical question (precision medicine, checkpoint blockade) proceed by biological mechanism and then by resistance and combination strategy.

## Thesis-per-section convention

Sections in medicine reviews characteristically open with a claim about the clinical or mechanistic phenomenon rather than an orientation sentence. The first sentence is expected to do work. "PD-L1 expression on tumor cells is an imperfect biomarker: it correlates with response across tumor types but is neither sensitive nor specific enough to guide treatment decisions reliably" (Ribas & Wolchok, Science 2018, §4). "Cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity are the two major dose-limiting toxicities of CAR T cell therapy, both attributable to the power of the immune response that these living drugs unleash" (June et al., Science 2018, §4). "Lipid nanoparticles are currently the most clinically advanced mRNA delivery system, with ionizable lipid formulations enabling efficient hepatic and non-hepatic delivery depending on lipid composition and particle size" (Pardi et al., Nat. Rev. Drug Discov. 2018, §5). Each of these sentences states a qualified but definite claim — not 'in this section we review delivery systems' — and opens the analytical argument before any citation arrives.

## Synthesis moves

The five canonical synthesis techniques in medicine reviews are as follows.

The **contrast-pair structure** is the most distinctive. Reviews in this domain repeatedly organize their central argument around two opposed entities: CTLA-4 versus PD-1 as mechanistically complementary checkpoints (Ribas & Wolchok, Science 2018), haematological versus solid tumour settings as the defining binary of the CAR T field (Hou et al., Nat. Rev. Drug Discov. 2021), unmodified versus nucleoside-modified mRNA as the master variable explaining why the mRNA vaccine platform stalled and then accelerated (Pardi et al., Nat. Rev. Drug Discov. 2018). The contrast pair does double duty: it organizes the section architecture and generates an implicit argument about mechanism.

The **comparative evidence table** absorbs citation-dense content. Rather than narrating clinical trials sequentially, top medicine reviews tabulate them by target, tumour type, response rate, and approval status. Both Nature Reviews Drug Discovery reviews in this corpus carry two or three such tables (Hou et al., 2021; Pardi et al., 2018), and Topol (Nat. Med. 2019) uses three. The table permits claim-led prose to focus on interpretation rather than enumeration.

The **taxonomy tree** provides organizational logic for heterogeneous applications. Ashley (Nat. Rev. Genet. 2016) organizes precision medicine applications by genomic complexity (monogenic → polygenic → pharmacogenomic). Pardi et al. (2018) divide mRNA vaccine applications into prophylactic and therapeutic, then subdivide therapeutic by antigen type. June et al. (Science 2018) classify engineering strategies into intrinsic, extrinsic, and manufacturing categories. In each case the taxonomy appears before the application sections, so it functions as a navigation map rather than a retrospective summary.

The **running example** anchors abstract claims in a concrete case. Rajkomar et al. (NEJM 2019) return to their opening patient vignette in the conclusion, making the review's argument visible as a clinical before-and-after. Ashley (Nat. Rev. Genet. 2016) uses cystic fibrosis CFTR mutations as a recurring exemplar of the general precision medicine paradigm throughout the review.

The **historical arc as causal argument** transforms prior failure into mechanistic prerequisite. Ribas & Wolchok (Science 2018) trace Coley through cytokine therapies to checkpoint discovery not to document history but to argue that checkpoint blockade required both the failure of non-specific immunostimulation and the molecular dissection of T cell inhibitory pathways.

## Transition moves

Medicine reviews move between topics through a small repertoire of explicit connective gestures. "Translating these successes from hematologic malignancies to solid tumors requires confronting a qualitatively different set of biological barriers" (June et al., Science 2018, §2→§3) — contrast. "Even with an ideal target antigen, CAR T cells that cannot physically reach the tumour will fail" (Hou et al., Nat. Rev. Drug Discov. 2021, §2→§3) — caveat that carries momentum forward. "The pattern-recognition advantage that deep learning demonstrates in radiology extends, with equal force, to the domain of digital pathology" (Topol, Nat. Med. 2019, within §1) — bridge that imports the preceding argument into a new domain. "Understanding these resistance mechanisms has directly informed the design of rational combination strategies" (Ribas & Wolchok, Science 2018, §5→§6) — causal bridge that makes the section transition feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. "Perhaps the most underappreciated dimension of AI in medicine is its potential to engage patients more directly in their own health management" (Topol, Nat. Med. 2019, §2→§3) — zoom-in that shifts register from institutional to individual.

## Citation voice

Medicine reviews are parenthetical-dominant: numbered references or author-year parentheticals follow claim sentences rather than leading them. The typical paragraph carries two to four citations, with the introduction and the clinical evidence sections running slightly denser. Named citations in prose — "Maude et al. reported," "Hodi et al. published" — are reserved for landmark findings that changed clinical practice or for the authors' own foundational work. In NEJM reviews, named citations in prose are rarer than in Science or Nature Medicine; the NEJM house style tends toward passive constructions ("response rates of 81% have been reported") that make the finding rather than the finder the subject of the sentence. Author name in prose is more common at the beginning of review sections describing the history of a field than in analytical body sections, where parenthetical citation allows the claim to carry the sentence.

## Anti-stacking discipline

The best medicine reviews consistently open paragraphs with claims about the phenomenon rather than with the name of the group that studied it. The median claim-leading fraction across the corpus is approximately 60%, with authorship-leading openings running at 15–25%. "Antigen heterogeneity is a fundamental challenge in solid tumours: unlike CD19 on B cell malignancies, most solid tumour-associated antigens are expressed on only a subset of tumour cells, allowing antigen-negative cells to escape CAR T cell killing" (Hou et al., Nat. Rev. Drug Discov. 2021) — the author names do not appear until a parenthetical citation two sentences later. "Prognosis — predicting what will happen to a patient — is an area in which machine learning has shown particular promise" (Rajkomar et al., NEJM 2019) — the subsection opens with the clinical task, not the computational researchers. "Nucleoside modification — specifically the substitution of uridine with N1-methylpseudouridine — reduces innate immune activation and increases protein expression by an order of magnitude compared with unmodified mRNA" (Pardi et al., Nat. Rev. Drug Discov. 2018) — the finding is stated, and Karikó et al. (who made the discovery) appear parenthetically in the following sentence. The stacking alternative looks like this: "Maude et al. reported that tisagenlecleucel produced a complete remission rate of 81% in pediatric and young adult patients with relapsed or refractory B cell ALL" (June & Sadelain, NEJM 2018) — the author leads and the finding follows. This construction is not absent from top reviews, but it is confined to sections where the author's identity is clinically or historically significant (a landmark trial, a regulatory milestone) and never appears as the default paragraph opener.

## Figure and equation roles

Medicine reviews carry no equations. Mathematics is almost entirely absent from the domain; quantitative claims are made in prose or in tables. Figures serve four principal functions: the overview schematic (CAR structure, mRNA intracellular fate, cancer immunity cycle), the conceptual framework (the self-driving car autonomy analogy in Topol, the precision medicine opportunity landscape in Ashley), the mechanism-of-action diagram (CRS cascade, checkpoint engagement at priming versus effector phase), and the platform comparison figure. Boxes appear in longer Nature Reviews papers and perform definitional work — distinguishing precision medicine from personalized medicine (Ashley, Nat. Rev. Genet. 2016), distinguishing CAR T cells from TCR-engineered T cells (Hou et al., 2021) — without interrupting the flow of the main argument. NEJM and Science reviews carry no boxes. Tables substitute for figures in clinical evidence sections: response rates, approval dates, toxicity grades, and trial phases are tabulated rather than plotted.

## Signature moves

1. Open with the clinical stakes before the mechanism: establish what patients cannot be helped by current therapy, then introduce the technology as the candidate solution — not the reverse.
2. Use the contrast pair as the structural spine: identify the two states, conditions, or mechanisms that define the central tension of the field, and let that binary organize the section sequence.
3. Absorb clinical trial evidence into tables; reserve prose for mechanistic interpretation and cross-trial inference that the table cannot perform.
4. Name authors in prose only for landmark findings that require attribution to a person rather than a parenthetical number — treat named citation as a rhetorical gesture of historical elevation, not a default citation style.
5. State the obstacle before the engineering solution: every new section that introduces a problem should open with a claim about why the problem is hard before offering the solution, sustaining analytic credibility rather than promotional momentum.
