# Transition moves — the grammar of movement

Transitions between sections (and between paragraphs within a section) are
where the stacker most often slips. "Next, we discuss…" is the tell. A top
review names the bridge — cause/effect, contrast, zoom-in, caveat, historical
pivot — with a transitional sentence that carries information.

## The five transition functions

### 1. Bridge (cause → effect)

"Having established X, we now turn to Y, where the same framework explains…"
— used when §N+1 builds directly on §N's result.

- **Template:** `<Result established>, which opens the question of <new topic
  of §N+1>.`
- **Example (physics):** "The blockade mechanism developed in §2 fixes the
  two-body interaction; what remains is to understand how the many-body
  collective state emerges, which we take up in §3."

### 2. Contrast

"Where X works by A, Y works by B." — used when §N+1 is a different way of
attacking the same problem.

- **Template:** `Whereas <§N approach> relies on <mechanism>, <§N+1 approach>
  instead <alternative mechanism>.`
- **Example (materials):** "Whereas the van der Waals heterostructures of §3
  rely on mechanical stacking, twisted bilayer systems generate their
  electronic structure from a single monolayer rotated against itself."

### 3. Zoom-in (or zoom-out)

Move between scales. Used when §N gives a landscape and §N+1 does a deep
dive, or vice versa.

- **Template:** `Within <broad category>, the case of <specific> is
  instructive because <reason>.`
- **Example (economics):** "The automation framework of §2 generalizes over
  industries; §3 drills into one that has been studied in unusual detail —
  US manufacturing between 1990 and 2015."

### 4. Caveat / objection

Acknowledge a complication before proceeding. Used when §N has glossed over
something §N+1 must address.

- **Template:** `The preceding argument assumes <assumption>. When <condition>,
  however, <new phenomenon> arises; we take this up in §N+1.`
- **Example (medicine):** "The efficacy of CAR-T in hematologic malignancies,
  demonstrated across dozens of trials (§2), does not translate directly to
  solid tumors — as the following section documents, the microenvironmental
  barriers are qualitatively different."

### 5. Historical pivot

Temporal movement. Used when §N+1 introduces a new era that supersedes or
re-examines §N's framing.

- **Template:** `This picture persisted until <event / year>, when <shift>
  required the framework we develop next.`
- **Example (astronomy):** "For two decades the consensus held that galaxy
  formation was driven primarily by baryonic physics. The simulations of the
  last five years, which we turn to next, have revised this view
  fundamentally."

## Paragraph-to-paragraph transitions (within a section)

Within a section the transitions are finer-grained. Common patterns:

- **Refinement:** "A further wrinkle is…" / "More precisely…"
- **Evidence:** "This is not speculation — X (cite) measured…"
- **Consequence:** "It follows that…"
- **Comparison:** "In the competing picture…"
- **Deepening:** "The mechanism behind this is…"

Avoid at all costs:
- "Next,…"
- "Also,…"
- "Moreover,…" as paragraph opener (mid-sentence is fine)
- "Another paper (Smith 2019) showed…"

## Quality check

Before finalizing a section:

1. Read the first sentence of every paragraph in sequence.
2. Can you reconstruct the section's argument from just those sentences?
3. If yes, the transitions are doing their job. If no, the section stacks —
   rewrite paragraph openers.

## The one move that always works

When you cannot think of a transition, state the claim the next section will
prove, then end the current paragraph. The next section's header picks up the
baton.

> "…which leaves one question: how does the same framework extend to
> three-body interactions?
>
> ## 4. Three-body interactions and the emergence of topological order"

This works because it reassures the reader that the review has direction,
without narrating the direction ("In this section we will…").
