# High-Impact Journal Writing Style

Applies to: **Nature, Science, Cell, PNAS, eLife, Nature sub-journals**

Load this when the target venue is a broad-audience top journal.

---

## Fundamental Difference from Conference Papers

High-impact journals demand writing for a **broad scientific audience**, not just specialists. A Nature paper should be understandable (at least the intro and abstract) by scientists outside your field.

| Aspect | Conference Paper | High-Impact Journal |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Audience** | Specialists in subfield | All scientists |
| **Tone** | Technical, method-focused | Story-driven, significance-focused |
| **Abstract** | Can be technical | Must be non-technical (Nature: 150 words) |
| **Introduction** | Jump to technical gap quickly | Start broad, narrow gradually |
| **Methods** | In main text | Often separated (Online Methods) |
| **Contribution** | Novel method/algorithm | Scientific discovery or insight |
| **Page limit** | Strict (7–12 pages) | Word count (often ~3,000 main text) |
| **Figures** | Supporting evidence | **Central narrative device** |

## Writing for a Broad Audience

### The "Cocktail Party Test"
Can you explain your paper's significance to an intelligent non-specialist at a cocktail party? If not, your introduction needs work.

### Introduction Structure (Nature-style)
1. **Paragraph 1**: Broad context — why does this field matter to science? (accessible to all)
2. **Paragraph 2**: What's known — key prior work, established understanding
3. **Paragraph 3**: What's unknown — the gap, the question
4. **Paragraph 4**: What we did — "Here we show/report/demonstrate..."
5. **Paragraph 5** (optional): Brief results preview

**Key difference**: No bullet contribution list. The narrative flows naturally from gap → approach → finding.

### Avoid Jargon in Opening
- ❌ "We propose a novel graph neural network with equivariant message passing..."
- ✅ "Predicting how proteins fold remains one of biology's grand challenges. Here we report..."

## Abstract Writing (Nature/Science/Cell)

### Nature (150 words, "Bold First Paragraph")
- Start with general significance (1–2 sentences)
- State what you did ("Here we show/report/demonstrate...")
- Key finding (the actual scientific result)
- Implication (why it matters for the field)
- **No references**, no jargon, no acronyms
- Must be self-contained for a non-specialist

### Science (125 words preferred, 250 max)
- No citations, no undefined abbreviations
- One paragraph, no breaks

### Cell (150 words, "Summary")
- No references, no abbreviations (unless universally known)
- Accessible to broad readership

### PNAS (250 words + Significance Statement)
- The 120-word **Significance Statement** is unique to PNAS
- Written for a general audience — explain why this work matters to science broadly
- Most common rejection cause for PNAS: Significance Statement too technical

## Figure Strategy

In Nature/Science/Cell, **figures tell the story**. Reviewers often look at figures before reading the text.

### Figure 1 is Everything
- Should communicate the core idea visually
- Often a schematic/overview + key result combined
- Must be understandable without reading the paper

### Nature-Specific Figure Rules
- No "Scheme" numbering — use "Figure" for everything
- Scale bars, not magnification factors
- Error bars must be defined in caption
- Extended Data: up to 10 additional figures (peer-reviewed, online only)

### Cell-Specific
- Graphical Abstract: exactly **1200×1200 px**, 300 DPI
- 3–4 Highlights: max **85 characters each** (very strict)

## Significance Framing

High-impact journals care about **"So what?"** more than technical novelty.

### Weak (method-focused)
❌ "We develop a new algorithm that improves prediction accuracy by 5%"

### Strong (significance-focused)
✅ "Our results reveal that [phenomenon], overturning the prevailing view that [prior assumption], with implications for [broader field]"

### The 3 Levels of Significance
1. **Field-specific**: Advances understanding within your subfield
2. **Cross-disciplinary**: Opens new questions in adjacent fields
3. **Societal**: Potential impact on technology, health, policy

Nature/Science/Cell want at least level 2; ideally level 3.

---

## Methods: Separate and Concise

- Nature family: Online Methods (up to ~3,000 words), published online only
- Science: Brief in main text; full in Supplementary Materials
- Cell: STAR Methods (no word limit) with Key Resources Table

### Golden Rule
The main text should be **readable without the Methods**. A reader should understand what you found and why it matters from the main text alone.

## Reference Style Notes

| Journal | Refs in Abstract? | "et al." Threshold | Article Titles? |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| Nature | ❌ Never | ≥6 authors | Yes |
| Science | ❌ Never | **List ALL authors** | Yes |
| Cell | ❌ Never | ≥10 authors | Yes |
| PNAS | OK (sparingly) | ≥6 authors | Yes |

**Science** is unique: reference list must include ALL authors (never "et al." in refs).

## Common Pitfalls for Conference Authors Submitting to Journals

1. **Writing too technically** — Introduction must be accessible
2. **Method in main text** — Move to Online Methods / Supplementary
3. **Bullet contribution lists** — Journals use narrative flow instead
4. **Incremental framing** — "We improve X by Y%" → reframe as scientific insight
5. **Too many figures** — Nature: max 6; concentrate your story
6. **Missing required statements** — Data Availability, Author Contributions, Competing Interests
7. **Title too long/technical** — Nature: max 10 words; Science: max 96 characters
